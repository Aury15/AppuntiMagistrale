## What Is Dependability? Definitions & Attributes 

A **system** is an entity that interacts with other entities—including hardware, software, humans, and the physical world with its natural phenomena.

> [!definition] Dependability 
> **Dependability** is the ability of a system to deliver a service that can **justifiably be trusted**.
>
> Equivalently, a **service failure** occurs when the delivered service deviates from **correct service** (implementing its specification in both *functionality* and *performance*). Dependability is therefore the ability to avoid service failures that are **more frequent and more severe than is acceptable**.

Evaluating dependability requires combining **Performance & Workload** modeling (turning empirical system logs into random variables and probability distributions) with failure analysis.

Dependability is decomposed into five primary attributes and one secondary attribute:
- **Availability**: readiness of correct service (operational when needed).
- **Reliability**: continuity of correct service over time.
- **Safety**: absence of catastrophic consequences on users and the environment.
- **Integrity**: absence of improper (unauthorized) system alterations.
- **Maintainability**: ability to undergo modifications and repairs.
- **Robustness** *(secondary)*: dependability with respect to external faults, characterizing how a system reacts to a specific class of faults.

## Failures, Errors, Faults & Means to Attain Dependability 

When a system transitions from **Correct Service** to **Incorrect Service**, a **service outage** occurs until **restoration** brings it back online. Designing a dependable and secure system requires understanding the causal chain behind outages:

![[DDS_DEP1_07_service_failure.png]]

> [!important] The Threat Chain: Fault $\to$ Error $\to$ Failure 
> - **Fault**: the adjudged or hypothesized *cause* of an error (can be internal or external to the system).
> - **Error**: a *deviation* of the system's internal state from the correct service state, caused by a fault.
> - **Failure**: an *observable deviation* of the delivered service from its specification, generated by an error.

![[DDS_DEP1_09_fault_error_failure.png]]

### The Four Means to Attain Dependability 
1. **Fault Prevention**: prevent the occurrence or introduction of faults.
2. **Fault Tolerance**: avoid service failures even in the presence of faults.
3. **Fault Removal**: reduce the number and severity of faults (e.g., testing, debugging).
4. **Fault Forecasting**: estimate the present number, future incidence, and likely consequences of faults (connecting to reactive, preventive, and predictive maintenance).

Fault Prevention and Fault Tolerance aim to **deliver a trustworthy service**, while Fault Removal and Fault Forecasting aim to **justify** that the system meets its functional, dependability, and security requirements.

![[DDS_DEP1_10_means.png]]

### System Life Cycle Threats 
A system's life cycle has two main phases—**Development** (requirements, analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment, maintenance/review) and **Use** (service delivery, outages, shutdown). Development faults may be introduced by the environment (physical world, human developers, tools, or test facilities) and later manifest during use.

![[DDS_DEP1_12_lifecycle.png]]

## Distributed Systems Challenges: Heterogeneity, Openness & Security 

Building **Dependable Distributed Systems** introduces seven core challenges: *Heterogeneity, Openness, Security, Scalability, Fault Tolerance, Concurrency,* and *Transparency*.

### Heterogeneity 
Distributed systems span diverse **networks, hardware, operating systems, programming languages**, and **developer implementations**. Common solutions include **Middleware** (from RPC to Service-Oriented Architectures) and **mobile code / Virtual Machines** (e.g., JVM, EVM).

### Openness 
**Openness** is a system's capability to be extended and re-implemented, enabled by:
- **Complete, neutral Specifications** (describing behavior without dictating implementation details).
- **Well-defined Interfaces** (syntax, semantics, available functions, parameters, exceptions—often via an **IDL**, Interface Definition Language).

Openness encompasses six key sub-properties:

| Property | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Interoperability** | Two systems cooperate using components specified by a common standard |
| **Portability** | A component built on system A works on system B without modification |
| **Flexibility** | System can configure/orchestrate components from different programmers |
| **Add-on Features** | New components/services can be integrated into a running system |
| **Evolvability** | System can evolve over time (e.g., keeping two versions of a service active) |
| **Self-\*** | System can reconfigure and manage itself without human intervention |
### Security & Overlap with Dependability 
Security comprises three primary attributes—**Confidentiality** (no unauthorized disclosure), **Integrity** (no unauthorized alteration), and **Availability** (for authorized actions only)—plus three secondary attributes:
- **Accountability**: availability and integrity of the identity of who performed an operation.
- **Authenticity**: integrity of message content, origin, and metadata (e.g., emission time).
- **Non-repudiability**: availability and integrity of sender/receiver identities so neither can deny an action.

Notice how **Availability** and **Integrity** sit directly at the intersection of **Dependability** and **Security** (with Reliability, Safety, and Maintainability on the dependability side, and Confidentiality on the security side).

##  Scalability, Concurrency & Transparency 

### Scalability & Replication 
A system is **scalable** if it maintains adequate performance even as the number of users or resources grows by orders of magnitude. **Centralization is fundamentally against scalability**; the primary solution is **Replication**.

![[DDS_DEP1_20_scalability_centralized.png]]
![[DDS_DEP1_21_scalability_replication.png]]

However, replication introduces new distributed challenges:
- **Service** $\to$ coordination problems.
- **Data** $\to$ consistency problems.
- **Computation** $\to$ no node has the global state, nodes decide using local data, and single-node failures must not compromise the algorithm.

Designing a scalable system requires addressing four main issues:
1. **System Dynamicity**: adding/removing servers or processes on the fly.
2. **Checking Performance Metrics**: servers must not interact with all users or rely on algorithms requiring the entire dataset.
3. **Careful Use of Scarce Resources**: e.g., battery drain in embedded/IoT devices.
4. **Avoiding Bottlenecks**: e.g., centralized vs. distributed DNS (while noting that security or business rules may still mandate centralization in some deployments).

### Concurrency 
When multiple clients concurrently read and write shared resources, **coordination** and **synchronization** are required to ensure well-defined return values and prevent race conditions.

### Transparency 
**Transparency** hides the distributed nature of the system from users and developers across six dimensions:
- **Access**: access local and remote resources with the same operations.
- **Location**: access resources without knowing their physical location.
- **Concurrency**: share resources concurrently without mutual interference.
- **Failures**: mask failures so remaining operations can complete.
- **Mobility**: move resources and users without disrupting operations.
- **Performance**: reconfigure the system seamlessly under changing load.

*(Important caveat: a distributed solution does not always outperform a centralized one—simple models help manage this complexity.)*
